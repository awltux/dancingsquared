# Caller and teacher notes

Notes for the person running the evening: what to plan, what to track, and what to do about the calls
that keep failing.

## Planning an evening

Plan backwards from the get-out. You want a figure that finishes cleanly, built from calls the class
can already dance, so decide what that is first and build the evening towards it.

A ninety-minute evening works best when you alternate dancing and teaching, and when the new material
sits in the middle rather than at the end. The shape suggested in
[Teaching a class](../05-course/teaching-a-class.md) — a warm-up of known calls, the new work, a
break, then a full tip — is worth following until you have a reason not to.

Two rules are worth more than any plan:

- **Never teach past the point where the class stops improving.** If the call is not landing after
  fifteen minutes, park it and bring it back next week. A call taught twice badly is worse than a call
  taught once properly.
- **Write down what you taught.** Memory of a class is unreliable after a fortnight, and a class that
  repeats a call it did three weeks ago is a class that loses confidence.

## Tracking what a class knows

Keep a list of the units a group has been taught, in order, and mark each one:

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| taught | they have seen it and walked it |
| danced | they have done it to music, in a figure |
| secure | they can do it after a week off, without a reminder |

The jump from *taught* to *danced* is the one that matters, and the jump from *danced* to *secure* is
the one teachers most often skip. Building on a call that has only ever been walked is building on
sand.

This is where a written class record earns its place. For each evening, note which calls were
*introduced*, which were *walked*, and which were *danced to music* — three columns, and the third one
is the only one that counts. Keep it beside the music and the next class plans itself. You do not need
to invent a system: the three words above are the whole of it.

## Revising calls that keep failing

Some calls fail for weeks. When one does, the fault is usually one of four things:

1. **The class never learned the shape it starts from.** If dancers cannot picture the formation, no
   amount of repetition will help. Go back and dance the formation itself.
2. **The handhold is wrong.** Beginner errors are hands far more often than feet. Watch the hands on
   the first beat of the call, not the last.
3. **It was taught too late in an evening.** Calls taught when people are tired are learned badly.
   Teach it again, earlier, in a fresh evening.
4. **It only ever appears in one figure.** Dancers learn calls by meeting them in different places.
   Put the failing call into three different drills and let them meet it cold.

When you revise, revise **one** thing. Take the call back to its starting formation, walk it without
music, then dance it in a short figure, then put it back in the tip it came from. If it still fails,
stop teaching it that term and use the calls that work.

## Building a tip the class can dance

A tip is only as good as the calls in it. For a beginner class, a tip should use:

- calls the class has danced, not merely met;
- no more than one call from the most recent evening;
- a short opener, a figure, a break, and the standard get-out.

The get-out — Allemande Left, Right and Left Grand, Promenade Home — should be the same every week
until it is automatic. It is the one piece of a tip that dancers should never have to think about.

[Drills](../06-practice/drills.md) has a graded set you can lift figures from, and
[How to practise](../06-practice/how-to-practise.md) is what to hand a dancer who asks what they can
do between classes.

## Keeping the record honest

If the record says a call is danced and the square cannot dance it, the record is wrong. Fix it at the
end of the evening, while you can still remember which call it was. A teaching record that has drifted
from the class is worse than no record at all, because it will be trusted.
